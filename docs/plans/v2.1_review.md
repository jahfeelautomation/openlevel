**Reviewed documents**

- `OPENLEVEL_DBOS_FLY_NEON_ARCHITECTURE_V2(1).md` — 980 lines, supplied with this review request.
- `v2_review.md` — DeepSeek review and Muse follow-up review, 408 lines.
- Prior V1 review findings as summarized and cross-referenced in `v2_review.md`.

**Review date:** 14 November 2026  
**Review scope:** Architecture, technical correctness, implementation readiness, commercial viability, and compliance with the V2 directive.

**Repository limitation:** This review evaluates the supplied architecture and review documents. I did not inspect the local OpenLevel checkout or rerun Muse's repository counts, tests, or source searches. Repository-specific facts and counts below remain claims from the V2 investigation until independently reproduced.

## Executive verdict

**V2 is a materially better architecture than V1. It is not yet an implementation-ready controlling specification.**

The strategic simplification is sound: one Hono web tier using `DBOSClient`, one DBOS worker initially, one Neon database with application and DBOS schemas, transactional enqueue instead of a custom outbox pump, and a deliberate commercial foundation before the durable-workflow cutover.

V2 has also addressed most of the substantive V1 criticisms. It removes the dual-write bridge, the 5-second outbox pump, the custom DLQ and admin surface, the web-tier DBOS runtime, the arbitrary 24-hour drain cap, and the unfairly narrow pg-boss comparison. It correctly makes recovery, versioning, customer billing, tenant identity, and provider effects central architectural concerns.

But there are still material holes between the architecture's stated guarantees and its actual mechanics. The most important are:

1. The commercial and checkpoint economics are formulas, not the required scenario models.
2. The exact atomic-enqueue deduplication contract is not written down.
3. The one-worker executor identity is not demonstrably fenced by the Fly deployment configuration.
4. The AI-credit wallet does not yet bound platform spend across workflow recovery and uncertain model calls.
5. External provider effects are described as quarantined, but the replay path that prevents a duplicate call is not specified.
6. The connection budget omits or fails to break out DBOS client and datasource pools.
7. The traceability matrix remains behavior-group level rather than a complete, auditable launch-scope inventory.
8. Migration item 7, roadmap phases, product distribution, and several Day 2 procedures remain incomplete.

**Recommendation:** Approve V2 as the preferred architectural direction, but do not approve it as the implementation contract until the Critical findings below are closed. Do not reopen the whole architecture or restore V1's migration machinery. The remaining work is primarily to make the chosen design precise, bounded, and testable.

## 1. What V2 fixed correctly

The attached reviews are right to give V2 credit for the following changes. These are real corrections, not cosmetic edits.

- **No dual-write migration.** Strategy A is a clean-start recommendation with a written owner-confirmation gate. Strategy B is not built speculatively.
- **No outbox pump.** V2 recognizes that the application and DBOS system tables can live in one Postgres database and chooses `enqueueInTransaction` rather than a second polling state machine.
- **Web and worker are separated.** Hono uses `DBOSClient`; only the worker launches the DBOS runtime and dequeues work.
- **pg-boss is compared more fairly.** V2 acknowledges `startAfter`, transaction adapters, `singletonKey`, scheduling, retries, and retention. It no longer claims the original defects are impossible to fix in pg-boss.
- **Delayed continuation is considered.** V2 evaluates DBOS `delaySeconds` rather than assuming long `sleep()` calls are operationally free.
- **Transactional datasource is used selectively.** V2 does not claim ordinary SQL statements become exactly-once merely because they are inside a DBOS workflow.
- **Recovery and versioning are addressed.** The fixed executor identity, patching discipline, and Conductor trigger are a much clearer starting point than V1.
- **Provider-specific guarantees are separated.** Stripe, Square, Twilio, Brevo, Anthropic, Vapi, Chatwoot, and social publishing are not treated as if they all supported the same idempotency mechanism.
- **Neon restore semantics and connection arithmetic are corrected.** V2 no longer relies on the earlier separate-database/timeline argument and separates pooled clients from direct backends.
- **The commercial layer moves forward.** Agencies, subscriptions, AI credits, entitlements, and provider ownership are not left until after the durable-workflow migration.
- **No invented price points are carried over.** V2 correctly refuses to treat proposed $97/$297/$497 tiers as proven willingness to pay.
- **The operational surface is smaller.** Fly logs, Sentry, DBOS management, and a small set of recovery procedures are preferred over a bespoke operations console.

These decisions should remain the baseline. The corrections below should refine them, not undo them.

## 2. Corrections to the attached reviews

The attached DeepSeek and Muse reviews are useful, but not every sentence should be accepted as technically exact.

### 2.1 Atomic enqueue: distinguish workflow-ID reuse from queue deduplication

The DeepSeek review's C2 is directionally correct: V2 does not specify the complete handler behavior under duplicate business submissions. However, its explanation blurs two distinct DBOS controls.

Current DBOS TypeScript documentation distinguishes:

- `workflowID` plus `workflowIDReusePolicy`, which governs reuse of an existing workflow ID. The documented default for `workflowIDReusePolicy` is `return-existing`.
- `deduplicationID` plus `duplicationPolicy`, which governs queue-level deduplication. `enqueueInTransaction` specifically does not support `duplicationPolicy: 'return-existing'`.

Those are not interchangeable. V2's sentence that a stable business UUID becomes the workflow ID and therefore collapses duplicate enqueue attempts may be valid through workflow-ID reuse. It is not correct to infer that all `return-existing` behavior is unavailable simply because queue `duplicationPolicy` is unavailable.

**Corrected finding:** C2 remains a blocker because V2 does not say which mechanism it uses, whether it is relying on the default or setting it explicitly, how the business-row conflict is handled, or what the HTTP response returns. The review should not prescribe “duplicate workflow-ID error means success” as the only implementation; with `workflowIDReusePolicy: 'return-existing'`, the documented behavior is to return the existing workflow handle.

**Required correction:** Specify `workflowIDReusePolicy: 'return-existing'` explicitly for the stable workflow ID, distinguish it from queue `duplicationPolicy`, and document the complete transaction and conflict path. Prefer this business-event pattern:

1. Begin the transaction on the DBOS system-database connection.
2. Insert the durable business-event/dedup row using a unique business key and `ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING RETURNING`.
3. If newly inserted, enqueue exactly one workflow in the same transaction, using the stored event UUID as `workflowID` and explicit `workflowIDReusePolicy: 'return-existing'`.
4. If the business row already exists, do not create a second business event. Read its stored workflow ID and return its existing workflow status/handle; do not silently manufacture a new workflow because the original workflow history may have expired.
5. Commit the business row and DBOS enqueue atomically.
6. On transaction failure, roll back both. The client retries the same business key.

If the implementation instead always invokes `enqueueInTransaction` after `ON CONFLICT`, prove that the existing workflow ID is handled as intended under the pinned SDK. Do not rely on an unstated SDK default.

**Acceptance test:** Two concurrent duplicate submissions with the same provider/business event ID produce one business event, one workflow ID, one workflow execution, and zero duplicate provider effects. Also test a duplicate arriving after the workflow has completed and after workflow-history retention has removed the DBOS record.

### 2.2 Fly `immediate` deployment is not a fencing guarantee

The attached review correctly identifies the app-wide scope of Fly's `[env]` block: the sketch places `DBOS__VMID` in `[env]` while commenting that it is worker-only. That comment is not enforced by the configuration.

However, the attached review's proposed correction — set `[deploy] strategy = "immediate"` — must not be accepted as a complete fence. Fly describes `immediate` as updating Machines together and not waiting for them to start or pass health checks. That is not a documented stop-old-worker-before-start-new-worker guarantee.

**Corrected finding:** The VMID scoping defect is real, and the deployment/fencing design is incomplete. But `immediate` is not itself the solution.

**Required correction:**

- Set the executor identity only in the worker startup path. Use a worker-only boot wrapper or role-aware bootstrap that sets and validates `DBOS__VMID` before DBOS initialization. Do not place a worker identity in app-wide `[env]`.
- Specify the Fly restart policy for the worker process group.
- Specify the actual deploy procedure for the singleton worker: stop or fence the prior worker, verify it can no longer execute, then start the replacement with the same executor ID. The procedure must be automated or explicitly operator-gated; a manual runbook alone does not make routine deploys safe.
- Treat a one-worker deploy as a brief background-processing interruption, not as a zero-downtime worker rollout.
- Add a durable lease/fencing check if Fly Machine state alone cannot prove that an old executor is dead. A stale process must not continue making provider calls after a replacement has started.
- Preserve the executor ID and compatible application version on rollback.
- Test forced Machine replacement, deploy interruption, worker crash-loop, and attempted old/new overlap. A process restart test is insufficient.

### 2.3 `maxRecoveryAttempts` is not the same thing as step retry count

The attached C4 correctly identifies an AI-cost risk, but the wording should be precise. DBOS's workflow `maxRecoveryAttempts` controls how many times a workflow may be attempted during recovery; it is distinct from a step's own `maxAttempts` and `retriesAllowed` configuration.

That distinction does not remove the risk. A provider call may succeed externally, then the process can die before DBOS durably records the step result. Recovery may then re-execute the non-idempotent call. The default recovery ceiling therefore does not constitute a safe cost policy.

**Corrected finding:** The plan must bound both ordinary step retries and workflow recovery for money-spending AI operations, and it must explicitly handle a crash after provider success but before the result is checkpointed.

### 2.4 RLS should be explicitly deferred, not falsely demonstrated

V2's decision not to enable RLS at launch is defensible. The attached review is right that the statement about `SET LOCAL` compatibility is not a four-context proof and cites practitioner material rather than the official-source standard V2 claims to follow.

Do not add a large RLS implementation to the launch scope merely to satisfy a planning-document checkbox. Instead:

- Label transaction-pooling compatibility as **unverified**.
- State that RLS is **not enabled at launch**.
- Make the Phase 3 RLS pilot a gated experiment with an official PostgreSQL/Neon evidence note and tests across ordinary queries, DBOS transactional datasource calls, worker connections, and pooled connections.
- Document which privileged roles bypass policies and ensure the runtime path does not use those roles.

### 2.5 Appendix T should be complete for the declared scope, not necessarily 230 hand-written essays

The attached review correctly rejects a purely representative matrix. It should not, however, force a 230-row prose document if many endpoints are intentionally out of launch scope.

The right correction is a complete route inventory with an explicit disposition for every registered endpoint, then detailed behavior traceability for every launch-critical route/repository/integration. Mark each remaining route as retained-but-not-promoted, deferred, retired, or excluded. The matrix must not imply that route groups such as “contact CRUD” prove all endpoint contracts.

## 3. Critical findings — close before implementation approval

### C1. Required business and execution economics are not delivered

**Where:** §§6, 12–13; Appendix R item 18.

V2 provides a fixed-stack estimate and useful formulas, but defers the required scenario models to a future worksheet. That is not enough for a plan whose objective is a profitable SaaS, especially when AI calls are a major variable cost.

The document still needs:

- A scenario model for 10, 50, 100, 500, and 1,000 agencies.
- Separate BYO-messaging and platform-funded/managed-messaging scenarios.
- Explicit assumptions for locations per agency, seats, active contacts, AI turns, model mix, campaign volume, plan revenue, usage revenue, churn, support hours, payment fees, and provider usage.
- MRR, AI COGS, messaging COGS where applicable, Neon compute/storage/branch usage, Fly Machines, monitoring, DBOS checkpoints, payment processing, support cost, contribution margin, gross margin, and break-even agency count.
- Sensitivity cases for heavy AI users, unusually active campaigns, low usage, high support burden, and churn.
- Cost per 10,000 and 100,000 workflow executions for the pg-boss and DBOS options.
- Checkpoint volume at 10k, 100k, 1M, and 10M executions/month, with explicit placeholder multipliers for agent turns, automation journeys, campaign recipients, and import batches.

Do not invent benchmark numbers. Use a transparent placeholder scenario and label every input. Show the formula and result so the business can replace placeholders with paid-pilot data. Add a “not measured yet” column for checkpoint multipliers. If a value truly cannot be estimated, mark it as a blocker rather than claiming the model is complete.

The fixed-stack estimate is not the business model. It excludes or under-specifies the largest variable risks: model retries, heavy tenants, support time, payment disputes, and customer acquisition.

**Acceptance:** The document includes a reproducible spreadsheet-ready model, shows break-even under both provider-ownership scenarios, and identifies which unknowns must be measured in the first paid pilots.

### C2. Atomic enqueue and duplicate submission semantics are not implementable from the current prose

**Where:** §7.1, Appendix C, Appendix T.

The use of `enqueueInTransaction` is a good choice, but “stable UUID + `ON CONFLICT` + double-enqueue collapses” leaves the exact control flow ambiguous.

The plan must say whether it uses `workflowIDReusePolicy`, queue `deduplicationID`, or both. It must not treat their policies as synonyms. It must also define the duplicate business-row response, the workflow status returned to the API caller, and the behavior after DBOS execution history retention.

**Acceptance:** Add the exact handler pseudocode and the race/replay tests described in §2.1.

### C3. Single-worker recovery is a principle, not yet a proven Fly deployment contract

**Where:** §§5, 10–11; Appendix C.

The fixed executor ID is a reasonable launch strategy only if no two live processes can use it concurrently and the replacement can recover the old executor's work. The config sketch currently does not enforce worker-only VMID scope. No deploy strategy, restart policy, automated fencing hook, or worker shutdown sequence is specified.

`immediate` must not be presented as a sufficient fence. It does not document a stop-before-start guarantee.

**Required design:** Worker-only executor ID; explicit restart policy; serial stop/fence/start procedure; readiness/heartbeat signal; graceful shutdown behavior; deploy and rollback sequence; forced-replacement behavior; test proof.

**Graceful shutdown detail:** The worker must stop accepting/dequeueing new work, allow or safely interrupt in-flight steps within the kill timeout, close DBOS and database pools, and leave incomplete work recoverable. Setting `SIGTERM` and `kill_timeout=120s` does not implement that behavior by itself.

**Acceptance:** A deployment/replacement test proves that a previous executor cannot execute after the new worker assumes the same ID.

### C4. AI-credit reservation does not yet bound platform COGS

**Where:** §§8, 11–12, 16.

The wallet design prevents customers from using uncredited balance only if all spend is reserved and settled correctly. It does not automatically bound the platform's provider bill when a model call is non-idempotent and a successful call is replayed after a crash.

The present document does not define:

- A complete reservation state machine, such as `reserved → settled | refunded | expired`.
- A durable provider-call attempt identity separate from the customer billing operation identity.
- Whether each provider attempt reserves additional maximum cost or is covered by a hard per-workflow spend cap.
- How unknown outcomes are reconciled before retry.
- Reservation expiration and an orphan-reservation sweeper.
- The sweeper's cadence, locking, and idempotency.
- The exact period for which a wallet row lock is held.
- Per-workflow recovery limits and per-step retry limits for expensive model calls.
- The behavior when the provider succeeds but the response is lost before settlement.

The wallet row lock must be held only for a short reservation or settlement transaction — never across an LLM network call. Each attempt must be attributable and bounded. A recovery retry must not silently create unlimited platform spend merely because the customer was charged once.

**Required correction:** Define the ledger and provider-attempt state machines, an expiry/reconciliation job, a hard per-run cost budget, and retry limits. A retry must first inspect the attempt state and reconcile uncertain provider outcomes where possible. If the result cannot be reconciled, leave it uncertain and require a deliberate policy decision; do not retry indefinitely.

**Acceptance:** Crash tests cover (a) crash after reservation, before provider call; (b) crash during provider call; (c) provider success followed by process death before result checkpoint; and (d) concurrent turns exhausting the same wallet. Each test proves both customer-billing correctness and a hard platform-COGS ceiling.

### C5. External provider effects are not yet protected by a complete replay protocol

**Where:** §§7.2, 8, Appendix T.

V2 says “uncertain outcomes quarantine” and “a retry never blindly re-POSTs.” That is the right policy, but the actual recovery path is missing.

Consider a non-idempotent Twilio or Chatwoot send:

1. The workflow writes `pending`.
2. The provider accepts the request.
3. The worker dies before the provider response/result is checkpointed or before `outbound_operations` changes to `succeeded`.
4. DBOS recovers the workflow at the unfinished provider step.

At this point the database can still say `pending`. Unless the step checks the durable operation state and routes to reconciliation rather than calling the provider again, recovery can repeat the send. Conversely, refusing to retry every `pending` operation may strand a send that never reached the provider. The design must choose and explain the uncertain-outcome policy.

`outbound_operations` is an operation ledger, not necessarily a dispatch outbox. V2 should make that distinction explicit so it does not reject an outbox pump in §7.1 while relying on an undefined “outbox-record” in §8.

**Required correction:**

- Define a provider-operation state machine with explicit attempt IDs, timestamps, provider request/response IDs, request-body hash/version, and retry eligibility.
- Define which state is durably written immediately before the irreversible provider call.
- On replay, inspect the existing operation/attempt before any external call.
- For provider-enforced idempotency, reuse the exact stable key and identical request body within the provider's supported retention window.
- For providers without idempotency or lookup, do not promise exactly-once delivery. Quarantine uncertain outcomes and make the at-most-once/possible-loss trade-off explicit.
- Define how callbacks, provider lookup, operator resolution, and customer-visible status reconcile the record.
- Replace absolute Appendix T statements such as “exactly one POST” with a guarantee the mechanism can actually deliver.

**Acceptance:** For every provider, a crash after provider acceptance but before local success persistence cannot trigger an automatic duplicate call. The test must also prove what the customer sees when the outcome remains uncertain.

### C6. The launch wedge remains too broad to prove profitability quickly

**Where:** §2, §§12–13, §17.

The proposed ICP spans home services, real estate, and med spas. Those verticals have different lead flows, integrations, compliance burdens, sales cycles, and buying triggers. The seven-part “agency operating system” still resembles a smaller GoHighLevel rather than one sharply differentiated product.

The document says to secure 10 paid pilots, but it does not define a single vertical, a repeatable acquisition route, the paid pilot offer, or a measurable outcome that a buyer can compare with the cost of switching.

“Snapshots/templates” may become useful distribution or retention assets, but the plan has not shown that they are a moat or that they create meaningful switching cost.

**Required correction:** Pick one first vertical and one paid outcome. A plausible first offer is reliable speed-to-lead for a specific lead source: capture → assign → first response → booking → follow-up, with an agency's existing messaging account. Validate which exact workflow customers will pay for before broadening the product surface. Test acquisition through agency consultants, niche communities, integration partners, and direct outbound; treat each as a hypothesis, not an established channel. Track the time and risk of importing contacts, pipelines, forms, and automation definitions from the incumbent.

Do not promise GoHighLevel parity. Explain exactly why the chosen pilot customer would switch or add this product.

## 4. Major findings — resolve before production launch or assign a dated gate

### M1. Connection budget omits a documented DBOSClient pool

**Where:** §9.

The DBOS TypeScript client documentation states that `DBOSClient.create()` defaults `systemDatabasePoolSize` to 10 unless a custom `systemDatabasePool` is provided. V2 budgets the explicit web `enqueueInTransaction` connections but does not show a DBOSClient pool for the web Machines. With two web Machines, this can add up to 20 pool slots unless intentionally resized or shared.

The worker's “20 direct” line also bundles ordinary steps, datasource, and the DBOS system-database pool without showing the component pools separately.

**Required correction:** Show every pool separately by process and connection string: Hono/application read pool, web DBOSClient pool, web transaction pool, worker DBOS system pool, worker datasource pool, and migration/admin connections. For each, state maximum size, direct or pooled Neon endpoint, and number of Machines. Include idle/minimum connections and peak connection budget. Set explicit pool sizes instead of relying on SDK defaults.

Run `SHOW max_connections` on the selected Neon compute and compare the calculated peak plus operational headroom. Do not count PgBouncer client slots as direct Postgres backends. The document's arithmetic is improved, but it is not yet an evidence-backed budget.

### M2. Neon region and Fly region are not justified by customer evidence

**Where:** §5, §13, §18.

`iad` is justified using Arizona/North Carolina seed/schema references. Those are not evidence of target-customer geography or paid-pilot concentration. Neon region is not named.

**Required correction:** Make the target geography a commercial decision first, then select a supported Fly region and the nearest suitable Neon region together. Record region availability, network latency, data-residency implications, provider-region latency, and expected customer geography. If the first customers are US-based, explain why the chosen US region beats alternatives; if Southeast Asia is the first market, redo the region comparison rather than carrying over `iad`.

### M3. Chatwoot's hosting and operating ownership remain unknown

**Where:** §8.

V2 correctly identifies self-hosted Chatwoot as a meaningful operating burden, but never determines whether the current deployment is Chatwoot Cloud or self-hosted.

**Required correction:** Mark hosting as **Unknown** until the owner confirms it. Record who operates upgrades, PostgreSQL, Redis, Sidekiq, backups, monitoring, and incident response. Keep the current integration for the pilot if it is managed and reliable; if self-hosted, explicitly include its operating burden in the unit economics and decide whether it is a launch dependency or a later replacement candidate.

### M4. Delayed-continuation design lacks its domain lifecycle

**Where:** §7.4–7.5.

V2 chooses `delaySeconds` as the default but does not define the durable journey record, cancellation, rescheduling, expiry, or stale continuation behavior. It also asserts 100,000 parked journeys are a row-storage problem without a benchmark.

**Required correction:** Define the minimum journey record and unique continuation identity; specify which workflow writes it; define cancellation of both the domain journey and delayed workflow; define what happens when a contact, location, consent state, or automation definition changes; define maximum supported delay and rescheduling behavior; and define how stale continuations are detected and made harmless. Do not add a custom polling scheduler unless measurements show DBOS delayed workflows cannot meet the requirement.

Benchmark 10k, 50k, and 100k delayed journeys for storage growth, due-time dispatch lag, queue contention, and operational cost. Compare the observed result with `DBOS.sleep()` only if a real use case requires it. Otherwise remove the unexplained `<15 min` sleep rule and use one delay strategy.

### M5. Tenant partitioning is asserted to provide fairness without a fairness test

**Where:** §§4, 6–7.

DBOS partitioned queues can apply limits per partition and global limits across partitions. That is useful isolation and throttling, but the document should not equate per-partition limits automatically with a guaranteed fair scheduling algorithm. The pg-boss comparison also does not explain the available group-concurrency alternatives well enough to justify “manual, no partitions.”

**Required correction:** Describe the exact fairness property required: per-location concurrency cap, per-agency rate budget, maximum queue wait, or starvation prevention. Compare the DBOS partition mechanism and pg-boss grouping/concurrency against that specific property. Benchmark a noisy tenant against several quiet tenants and verify that one cannot monopolize worker capacity.

### M6. Queue concurrency values are arbitrary starting assumptions

**Where:** §7.5.

The initial agent, dispatch, and bulk values are labeled as assumptions and tied to connections, but not to Node memory, model-provider concurrency/RPM, downstream provider limits, or per-tenant fairness.

**Required correction:** Treat them as unconfigured until the first load-test. Document the derivation formula for each queue using the tightest applicable limit among memory, database connections, model provider concurrency, provider rate limits, and tenant entitlement. Keep a controlled configuration and measure backlog age, task duration, retries, and saturation before increasing concurrency.

### M7. Workflow versioning needs a simple compatibility taxonomy

**Where:** §10, ADR 6.

Pinned application version plus `DBOS.patch()` is a defensible single-worker strategy. The plan says “breaking step-order changes get patch markers,” but does not say how a developer decides whether a change is safe without a patch.

**Required correction:** Add a short code-change taxonomy based on DBOS's documented compatibility definition:

- **No patch required:** changes that do not change the workflow's durable step sequence or the meaning of already-recorded results.
- **Patch required:** changes that add, remove, reorder, or conditionally alter durable steps for workflows that may already be running, while preserving the old path for old histories.
- **New workflow/version required:** changes whose old and new semantics cannot safely coexist in one patched function or whose persisted arguments/results are incompatible.

Also state that helper-function edits are not automatically safe: if the helper changes durable operations or the meaning of a replayed step, it is a workflow compatibility change. Require a patch review and recovery test for every workflow change.

### M8. Roadmap phases are not implementation contracts

**Where:** §17.

Phase 1 compresses agency model, role enforcement, billing, wallet, plan gates, encrypted credentials, OAuth, onboarding, and usage dashboard into one large phase. Phase 3 is one paragraph. Required dependencies, acceptance tests, cost impact, and operating burden are not specified for every phase.

**Required correction:** Split Phase 1 at least into:

- **1A — Agency and billing foundation:** agency membership, location ownership, Stripe subscription lifecycle, entitlements, basic usage ledger, wallet reservation/settlement, and payment failure/dunning behavior.
- **1B — Provider onboarding and credentials:** encrypted credentials, OAuth connect/refresh/revoke, onboarding health checks, and tenant-facing setup flow.

For each phase provide: deliverables, dependencies, acceptance tests, customer value, incremental monthly/variable cost, operational burden, rollback, and definition of done. Do not make a later expansion item a hidden prerequisite for the paid pilot.

### M9. Product capability counts contradict one another

**Where:** §2 and Appendix R item 15.

The capability table presents five “retain” rows, four “improve” rows, four “defer” rows, and one combined “retire” row. Appendix R says seven wedge keeps, four deferred, and two retired, with campaigns and voice improved. These categories can be reconciled only if the document explains that “wedge keeps” are product journeys rather than table rows.

**Required correction:** Use one canonical disposition list and define the unit being counted: capability row, feature, or launch journey. Courses and communities should be either one combined retirement category or two distinct retired capabilities consistently. Add snapshots/templates to the same list rather than silently counting them in the wedge.

### M10. Migration item 7 is not a complete migration decision

**Where:** §15.

Most Strategy A migration items can be N/A with evidence if there is no live customer state. Item 7 is different: V2 says long-running waits are “adopt-if-present,” but does not prove that `workflow_runs` stores a wake timestamp, nor define adoption, script retry, reconciliation, or rollback.

**Required correction:** Either downgrade item 7 to **notify-only / no automatic adoption** and state that the old wake time cannot be reconstructed unless verified from the actual schema/data; or supply the five required fields:

1. Migration strategy.
2. Compatibility requirements, including exact old fields and due-time evidence.
3. Cutover behavior.
4. Recovery behavior if the adoption script crashes partway through.
5. Rollback behavior.

Add the actual `workflow_runs` schema facts to the evidence ledger. Do not call a row “adoptable” until its due time and business context can be reconstructed.

Also change §1's “no live production state was found” to “no evidence of live production state was found in the repository.” The owner confirmation gate is good, but repository evidence cannot prove that an external production database does not exist.

### M11. Day 2 alert thresholds and recovery actions are incomplete

**Where:** §14.

V2 lists several paging signals but gives concrete example thresholds only for backlog and HTTP p95. “Error-budget burn,” “stuck workflows,” “DB saturation,” and “wallet-settlement failures” need measurable initial thresholds and runbook actions.

**Required correction:** Add a proposed threshold and response for each paging signal, explicitly marked provisional until staging/load tests. Define worker liveness separately from web `/health`: a Machine can be `started` while DBOS is not listening to queues or cannot reach the system database. Add a worker heartbeat/readiness signal and alert on stale heartbeat, not merely Machine state.

`resumeWorkflow`, `forkWorkflow`, and `rewindWorkflow` need an explicit launch policy. If rewind is not needed at launch, say it is not used and restrict the operator runbook to supported resume/fork/cancel actions until tested.

### M12. Migration tool and DBOS datasource grants are not specified

**Where:** §§9–10.

The plan describes versioned `db/migrations/` and an advisory lock but names no migration tool or version. That is not reproducible enough for a coding agent. The `app` role is also described broadly as having runtime DML on both schemas without spelling out the exact DBOS and datasource permissions.

**Required correction:**

- Select and pin one migration tool, version, invocation, lock mechanism, and rollback policy.
- Name the order of application-schema migration, DBOS system-schema migration, and datasource `transaction_completion` initialization.
- Use the datasource's supported schema initialization/grant mechanism to give the runtime role only the required `transaction_completion` permissions; do not grant runtime DDL because it is convenient.
- Distinguish what the web role needs from what the worker needs. If keeping one shared runtime role is a deliberate simplification, explain and threat-model it.
- State the actual purpose of `readonly` at launch or remove it until it has a real consumer.

### M13. Security and compliance gaps remain in the messaging and AI path

**Where:** §§8, 11–12, 16.

The plan says BYO messaging leaves carrier registration, consent, and sender reputation with the customer. That does not remove the platform's need to enforce suppression, opt-out, and authorization invariants when it schedules or sends messages. V2 already mentions rechecking DND/consent during campaign batches; it needs to make the same guarantee apply to every workflow and agent-triggered send.

**Required correction:** Make consent, opt-out, quiet hours/timezone, sender authorization, and duplicate suppression mandatory checks immediately before each irreversible send. Store evidence and timestamps needed to explain why a send was allowed. Do not make “BYO” a reason to bypass platform guardrails. Choose a first vertical carefully: a med-spa launch may introduce sensitive health information and additional compliance obligations that a home-services pilot may not.

### M14. Credential decryption boundaries are not specified

**Where:** §11.

AES-GCM encrypted columns, key IDs, and rotation are a good starting point, but the plan does not state which process may decrypt credentials. The web process handles onboarding and OAuth callbacks; the worker performs provider calls.

**Required correction:** Define the trust boundary. Prefer that only the worker/provider-adapter path can decrypt credentials needed for outbound calls. Web callbacks should encrypt and store tokens without returning plaintext to general application paths. Bind ciphertext to tenant/provider context with authenticated additional data, audit credential reads/rotation/revocation, and test tenant-crossing access.

If web must decrypt for OAuth refresh or another flow, document the narrow exception and its threat implications.

## 7. Recommended architecture direction to preserve

Do not turn this review into a new architecture exercise. The best current direction remains:

- Hono web process using `DBOSClient` only.
- One DBOS worker initially, with a stable executor ID and an enforced single-worker deployment/recovery procedure.
- One Neon database with `app` and `dbos` schemas, direct connections where required for transactional enqueue, and explicitly budgeted pools.
- DBOS-only for durable journeys, agent turns, schedules, and bulk orchestration, unless measurement later shows a simple workload should use a simpler engine.
- `enqueueInTransaction` for atomic business write plus DBOS enqueue, with business-level dedup and explicit workflow-ID reuse behavior.
- DBOS transactional datasource only for database effects that need transaction-completion replay semantics.
- A durable provider-operation ledger plus provider-specific idempotency/reconciliation policies; never promise exactly-once external effects where the provider cannot support it.
- Agency subscription and entitlements, with an AI wallet that limits both customer usage and the platform's worst-case provider spend.
- BYO messaging for the initial commercial model, with mandatory platform-enforced consent, suppression, and send authorization.
- A narrow first vertical and measurable paid outcome before expanding the full agency suite.

**Business principle:** Build a product a specific agency will pay for, not a feature-complete GoHighLevel clone and not a migration museum. The architecture is useful only insofar as it makes that first paid workflow reliable, safe, supportable, and profitable.
